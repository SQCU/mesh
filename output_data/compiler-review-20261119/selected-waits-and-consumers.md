# Which compiler results reach their consumers? 19 November 2026

## Metal dependency and synchronization records

**A.** The completion solver gave each cross-call dependency an index into its access relation. Projecting instruction edges onto Metal dispatch parts discarded that index. Selecting an encoder wait also discarded the physical dispatch edge that justified it. A signature operation could name a fence without identifying the native instruction that used it. [Prior review](source-width-and-cross-call-access.md) · [Synchronization signature §1](../../design/synchronization-signature-change-request.md)

**B.** The projection now retains the original access indices for each dispatch-part pair. The scheduler writes its selected edge index into the wait operand alongside the source position and iteration delay. The signature names the emitted instruction. Given the matching rank image and an operation, `image.synchronization_source(op)` reads the selected edge for that execution and exposes the original access witnesses. Structural events and local physical hazards can have an empty set of cross-call causes. The emitter saves the wait operands and the projection with the current program. The executing GPU and bridge do the same work as before. [Projection](../../rdma/mesh_graph.py) · [Wait selection](../../../../metal-microbench/metal_dispatch.m) · [Emission](../../../../metal-microbench/metal_program.m) · [Query](../../../../compiler-emit/compiler_emit/metal_schedule.py)

On one Mac, reversing two independent branches' tail weights reversed their issue order and yielded zero cross-call access edges. One consumer of a split result exposed original edge 0. Two invocations of that split exposed edges 0 and 1 across 21 wait operations with selected dispatch edges over two executions. Fresh and reopened images agreed on each site, its selected edge, its conflicts, and the eager numerical results. For upstream Metal kernels, the compiler reads the selected entry's Apple LLVM IR from the emitted pipeline. Apple's disassembler agreed on the barrier, fence and atomic counts for all 14 entries in the two tested selections. [Public FX evaluation](../../../../compiler-emit/artifacts/joint-sharding-20261119/evaluate.py) · [Per-kernel results](../../../../metal-microbench/output_data/selected-kernel-synchronization-20261119/observation.md)

External DTrace matched seven operations within the compiled Metal body. Tracing the public callable also found its PyTorch input-stream wait, its external-buffer residency notification, and its wait for completion before returning. The compiler's signature covers the seven body operations. The monitor reports the additional public-call events as mismatches. The producer-stream wait orders writes made by a different queue. Removing it requires a source of equivalent ordering or proof that the supplied storage is already ready. [Trace, event identities and stacks](../../../../compiler-emit/artifacts/joint-sharding-20261119/public-metal-trace.md)

On the physical receiver, native site 34 named unrolled dispatch edge 4 (3→4) and original access edge 1 (14→17). Two counterbalanced sweeps each sent changing 16 KiB pages through the Thunderbolt bridge. With twelve dependent GPU calls after the page reader, the next page landed before invocation end on 88 of 88 warmed reader-gated reposts and on zero of 88 end-gated reposts. The two reader-to-landing medians were 5.54 and 4.81 µs. These timings compare repost schedules. The remote hosts permitted recording bridge counters, GPU-published words and output tensors. The DTrace observation above ran locally. The physical signatures still flagged twelve cross-submission signal-to-wait pairs on each rank as lacking a matching dependency. ## Other consumers found in this review

| Producer result | Consumer behavior in the audited revision | Concrete effect |
|---|---|---|
| Route geometry supplies each cut's bytes, directed bandwidth and startup time. | R5 used a full-page weight for every cut. Changing startup altered routing operands while leaving its completion objective unchanged. | A 1,025-element float32 tensor produced a 4,096-byte transfer and a four-byte tail. R5 charged both the same service time. [R5](../../design/plan-to-bridge-change-request.md) · [Schedule construction](../../rdma/mesh-collective.c) |
| The scheduler orders instructions that share an exclusive resource. | The ordinary storage lifetime check consults the structural graph. The selected resource order has its own table. | For `x.sin().exp().tanh().square() + x.cos().abs().relu().sigmoid()`, one resource put the last read of the sine immediately before the first write of the absolute value. The allocator still used frames 1 and 4. Moving the second branch to another resource removed that ordering and gave the same two frames. Only an order enforced by the selected execution can justify sharing. [Lifetime consumer](../../../../compiler-emit/compiler_emit/storage.py) · [Resource order](../../rdma/mesh-collective.c) |
| FX provides both the shape and the stride of a tensor view. | Cross-processor placement constructs a contiguous clone and reconstructs the view on arrival before routing can compare transfer layouts. | For `x.sin()[:, ::2].cos()` with shape `(8, 2048)` and the cosine on rank 1, the graph acquired a clone, eight 4,096-byte sends, a receive and a scatter. Packing carries a real cost that a direct strided alternative could compete against. This example measures graph structure. [Placement](../../../../schedule-emit/schedule_emit/placement.py) |

The R5 correction in Mesh `b9f42c7a` and compiler-emit `c118f07` uses the actual cut length and the selected direction's bandwidth and startup. A hop occupies its link while it transmits bytes. A consumer or relay can use those bytes after the additional propagation delay. A second independent transfer can occupy the link during that delay. Both the function planner and the whole-program solver use these operands. In a nanosecond-weighted FX example, a 4,096-byte hop and its four-byte tail occupied the forward 80 Gb/s link for 409.6 and 0.4 ns. The reverse 40 Gb/s link took 819.2 and 0.8 ns. The forward and reverse directions added 70 and 900 ns to arrival, respectively. These are modeled times.
